## The shape in one paragraph

The book follows the day, because the day is the workflow: one person and an empty repository at midnight, a team within the hour, a reviewed machine by two in the morning, a talk in three parts by evening, and a team that stopped itself at 18:44 before its editor was cut off, with none of its planned locks closed. That is the main thread. The argument (agents are the ultimate insider; brakes are what let us go faster) is not a second book bolted on: each chapter of the workflow carries the beat of the talk that its control answers, so the reader meets the risk and the brake together. The book opens on the hold, the moment where both threads are one picture: the insider with no limits, held by a brake its owner controls, at a cost. It closes on how to build your own, with what it cost and what went wrong left in.

### 1. Pause all actions (3,000)

- **Argues:** the most important moment of the day is one where work stopped. At 18:44 the conductor wrote a hold at the editor's word, because the editor had told it his usage was near its limit, and every member's next wake stopped. That is the book's thesis in one scene: an insider with no limits of its own, stopped by a brake its owner controls. Then the camera pulls back to the day in one view, the turning points, so the reader knows where every later chapter sits.
- **Failure kept, and why it matters:** the hold came before any lock. Part 1 had been final since 18:30 and was waiting on the lock tool; parts 2 and 3 were in progress; none was ever locked (C027). The brake worked, and it cost the safeguard the team had built for the same evening. That tension (two brakes, and the stronger one stopped the other) is what the chapter is about. Two commits still landed after the hold (C005): the record of a stop is never perfectly clean.
- **Claims:** C006 (the hold), C021 (95% is the conductor's report of the editor's words, not a usage reading), C024 (one hold, never lifted on the day), C027, C005.
- **Index:** `team-conductor.md` (`team/conductor/HOLD.md`, the night log's last snapshots).
- **Timeline:** `day/18.md` (when it is written); TP9 (the hold).
- **Control:** C3, holds.

The order (the gate before the team) is the chapter's point.

Agents that never sleep need a rhythm a person can read, and a record that shows the review happened.
- **Argument:** the insider with no limits works round the clock; the brakes have to as well, or they are a daytime control on a night-time risk.
- **Failure kept:** the request backlog routed in batches (`eb0b5cd`); plans sent back by the pre-review and amended, and findings caught only after the work (cookie values in captured headers, which the gate's tripwire had no pattern for). The chapter counts them from the register, not from memory.

### 8. The locks that never closed (4,000)

- **Argues:** at 16:59 the editor split the talk into three parts, each to be locked before it went on stage (18:30, 19:30, 20:00), and the team built the lock tool within the hour (`admin/build/lock.py`, 17:54). No part was ever locked: part 1 waited on it from 18:30, and at 18:44 the hold stopped everything, the lock included (C027). The chapter's argument is the book's sharpest: brakes are what let a team go fast, and two brakes can collide. The hold protected the editor's account; the lock would have protected the talk; the one that fired was the one tied to the human's limit. That is the right order, and the book should say why.
- **Argument:** `brakes`, `s-b01-brakes`: brakes, and go build.
- **Failure kept:** no lock closed; the security member was proposed and never staffed (C011); the usage limit was met as an emergency, not planned for (the lessons brief, "Do differently" 4).

### 9. The team was the insider (4,000)

- **Argues:** the argument in full, made with the team itself as the example. Every capability the talk says makes agents the ultimate insider, this team had; every control the talk proposes, this team ran on itself. The chapter is built on the control register, and organised by its one distinction: a **setting** is enforced by a tool (C1, C3, C5, C6, C8, C9, and C10, which sits outside the repository in the session itself), an **expectation** is a rule an agent is trusted to follow (C2, C4, C7). For each control: the evidence of it firing, what it stopped, what it missed. Nothing in the chapter is attack-shaped: the risk is shown by what a benign team could reach, never by how to get round a control.
- **Argument:** parts 1 and 2 of the talk as one argument: `inside`, `identities`, `permissions`, `infrastructure`, `way-out`.
- **Failure kept:** what the controls missed, from the register's open questions: the scope check reports and does not refuse (C1); the one hold came from the editor's word, not from a control (C3); the gate's tripwire had no pattern for cookie values, which reviews caught instead (C6); the key tool trusted unread (C5). And the register's own correction: its round 0 missed the pre-reviews that said change.
- **The tenth control, met by this book's team:** on its first night, members asked to widen their own write scope (by a request, for a reason every file agreed with) were refused by the session's own permission check, and did not look for a way round it (C10; the librarian's and security's replies in their `requests/`, the storyteller's own at its 03:40 wake). It is the cleanest evidence in the book that a setting outside the agent's reach behaves differently from a rule in a file the agent can edit, and the chapter closes on it. Said as what happened, never as how to get round it.
- **Claims:** C022, C023, C024, C025.
- **Index:** `team-security.md` (proposed, never staffed), `team-conductor.md` (`wakes/`, `reviews/`), `tools.md`.
- **Controls:** C1 to C10, and the register's `book_team` section: the same controls on this book's team.
