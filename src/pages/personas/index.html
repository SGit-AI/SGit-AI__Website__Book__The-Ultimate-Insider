The briefing

By synthetic@book.the-ultimate-insider, 31 October 2026, from the eight exit interviews in review/synthetic/rc1/. Each persona read the RC1 chapters for its kind of reader (two or three chapters, one for the board member), from RC1 as locked (team/conductor/rc1/). Fictional readers: a prompt for the team, not evidence about the book.

Would they buy it?

All eight first personas said maybe, and nobody said yes or no. The reasons are the same in every voice: they trust it, and it is not yet what they would pay for. Trust scored 4 for every persona: the failures stay in, and the record is real. What holds them back is that the book is a record of one team's day more than something they can use, and that it does not say what the method costs.

What they would pay for is telling: a short PDF or briefing, a pack of templates and checklists, about 15 pounds as an ebook, or the book free online. Four of the eight named a short companion before the full book.

Where they get stuck

  • The opening of chapter 1. The board member, the founder, the journalist and the designer all stall on the first pages: lock times, commit hashes and register codes before the reader knows who the team is or why 18:44 matters. The board member "would have stopped there"; the founder "would have bounced".
  • The density of provenance. Hashes and claim numbers inline in the prose read as noise to the designer and the journalist, and the journalist cannot click through any of them to check.
  • Chapter 12 stops short of the how. The developer came for the recipe ("put every brake outside the agent's reach") and found a one-line pointer; the project manager could not plan the method because the editor's decision time is never given.

What is missing, by who asks

  • What it costs (CTO, founder, project manager, CISO): tokens, money, the editor's hours, and the time the brakes add. The CTO: "the number my whole decision turns on".
  • A kit (developer, founder, CTO, project manager): the real policy file, the checks, a CI job, a one-page checklist, a starter pack for a small team.
  • The so-what first (board member, journalist, CISO): one plain sentence of the finding at the top, and what to ask your own team.
  • Pictures (designer): a recurring timeline figure, and charts where chapter 7 has numbers.

What they will remember

The hold. Five of the eight name it: one file anyone can write, only the editor can lift, and the work already running still landed. The others remember its companions: the lock that was built and never run, and "a setting, not a boundary". The book's most memorable idea is already its opening scene; the problem is only that the scene opens with its paperwork.

Pace

Every persona read more than its budget allowed. Pace scored 2 for the board member, the founder, the project manager and the journalist, and 3 for the rest. See the reading-time proposal below.

The Gen ZX founders (added 1 November 2026)

Two personas added at the editor's word, to see the book from the Gen ZX Founders series' readers: a Gen Z founder (early twenties, a no-code app, a phone, 15 minutes) and a Gen X founder (early fifties, two decades in operations, starting a services company, a laptop, 30 minutes). Both read chapters 1 and 12, the same as the founder, so the three can be compared.

  • Both said maybe, for opposite reasons. The Gen Z founder: "I could not do anything from it tomorrow", because chapter 12's starting kit (a public repository, a scheduler, git) "told me this is not for someone building with an app builder". The Gen X founder: it cannot tell "whether the method is affordable or whether it survives without its one editor".
  • The Gen Z founder is the lowest scorer of the ten (answer 2, clarity 2, pace 2, buy 2). They followed the 18:44 story and stopped where the hashes and timestamps pile up; they had to look up conductor, wake, gate, tripwire and insider. They would share the hold story as a screenshot, not the book.
  • The Gen X founder read every word and scored like the founder (3s, trust 4). Their gap is the auditor's: no cost, no hours, and no named owner per control; the scope controls are "described as reporting rather than refusing, which an auditor would flag". They would pay 20 to 30 pounds, but only if cost and ownership are added.
  • What both want first: plain definitions of the team's words before they are used. What only the Gen Z founder asked for: a starter small enough for an evening with the tools they already have ("one agent, one rule file, one stop button").
  • What both remember: the hold. Ten personas now, and it is still the idea most of them carry away.

For the series, the read says RC1 as it stands is a Gen X book: the Gen X founder could use it with additions; the Gen Z founder is shut out by chapter 12's first page.

Limits of this test

One session's sub-agents voiced all eight, so the agreement is stronger than eight people would give: the eight identical "maybe"s and the eight 3s for buying say as much about the method as about the book. The differences between personas (what each misses, where each stopped) are the signal; the counts are not. Every stuck passage was checked against RC1 word for word: 45 quotes, one corrected from a paraphrase; the two Gen ZX founders' 14, all exact.

The ten-page sample, read by the room

SYNTHETIC READERS: test instruments, never readers. By synthetic@book.the-ultimate-insider, 1 November 2026. Six fictional readers like a room at a public day of building with AI (the editor's brief T10): a student, someone with an idea and no code, a local founder with customers, a mentor, and the Gen Z and Gen X founders. Each read manuscript/sample/first-ten-pages.md (about 2,000 words) from the top, on their own device, and answered the storyteller's test: does a reader who has never run an agent finish page ten knowing what the team did in a day, why it matters to them, and the first thing they would do tomorrow? The data, reader by reader, is in review/synthetic/sample/.

The test, passed in two parts of three

  • What the team did: passed by all six. Every reader could say it in one sentence, and the sentences agree: nine agents and one person, a talk built in a day, every step recorded, one file that could stop them all.
  • The first thing they would do tomorrow: passed by all six, and it is the same thing. Every reader would write the one page of rules for a first agent, with the sample's three headings (what it is for, what it may touch, when it stops). The page that asks for it works.
  • Why it matters to them: partly. The student, the Gen Z founder and the mentor found it; the idea-no-code reader, the local founder and the Gen X founder found the method but not their own case ("the book never shows an idea like mine"; "the pages never show a business task or a cost").

All six read to the end; on RC1, several personas stalled on chapter 1's first pages. The Gen Z founder, shut out by RC1's chapter 12, would now try it "tonight" in the app builder they already use.

Would they start tomorrow?

All six said maybe, and the reason is the same: step one. "Make an account" and "an empty repository" come with no how, and four of the six do not know where to begin; the local founder and the Gen X founder will not begin until they know what it costs.

What won them over

One line, named by all six: "Anyone on the team could stop everything. Only I could start it again." The hold is the idea every reader carries out of the sample, as it was in RC1. Second: "You do not need to write code to work like this" (the idea-no-code reader and the Gen Z founder).

Where they got stuck

  • The claim codes (C001 and the rest) in the middle of sentences: all six, and several say they break the story before it starts.
  • Words used before they are explained: conductor and wake (all six), Wardley maps (all six), hold, release, dev, the build. The "Words on this page" box helps, and the readers want it to cover these too.
  • Code on the page: the quoted command and the commit hash made the student, the idea-no-code reader and the Gen Z founder feel the book assumes they code (the Gen Z founder: it "looks like the book assumes I code").
  • The series paragraph's tone: the local founder found it delays the point; the Gen X founder read it as "hustle tone" that puts them off before the measured material.
  • No cost: the local founder and the Gen X founder, as on RC1.

For the storyteller and the publisher, in order

  • Move the claim codes out of the sentences on the first pages (to a note at the foot, or the "Where this came from" line).
  • Gloss conductor, wake, hold and Wardley maps in the "Words on this page" box, or leave them out of the first ten pages.
  • Give step one a how: one tool, one account, one empty project, in the reader's words, and say it works in an app builder too.
  • One example that is not a talk: a small business task, or a product idea, done the same way.
  • A sentence on cost, even a range.

Limits

One session's sub-agents voiced all six, so the agreement (six "maybe"s, the same first step) is stronger than six people would give. Where each stalled, and what each could not see for themselves, are the signal. Every quoted passage was checked against the sample word for word: 52 quotes, all exact.

Reading time: a proposal

A proposal for the editor, by synthetic@book.the-ultimate-insider, 31 October 2026. Asked in the session: "Are you thinking about reading time or your reading time?"

Two different times

  • The book's reading time is a property of the text: words divided by a reading pace. The review pages show it now ("about 52 minutes"), computed per chapter.
  • The reader's time is a property of the person: what a time-poor reviewer will actually give. The project README guesses forty minutes for everyone; the personas carry their own budgets (board member 10, journalist 20, founder 20, CTO 30, designer 30, project manager 30, CISO 40, developer 40).

The pages today ask for the book's time. The personas say that is the wrong way round: the CISO put the page off at 52 minutes, the founder at 33.

The proposal

  • The ask is set by the reader's time, not the book's. Each invitation asks for one chapter that fits inside twenty minutes, reading plus the questions, and names it: "read this one". Everything else is offered, not asked: "if you have more, these two".
  • Twenty minutes is the default ask for a real reviewer, because it is the founder's and the journalist's budget and the shortest any of the personas would give after the board member. A reader who gives more is a bonus, not the plan.
  • The board member is the exception that proves it. No chapter fits ten minutes (the shortest chapter in the review set, chapter 1, is about 3,300 words, thirteen minutes at 250 words a minute). For that reader the ask is a page, not a chapter: the opening of chapter 1 and the three questions. The editor may decide such readers are not asked at all.
  • Say the pace. Every time shown says how it was counted (words, and 250 words a minute), so a reader can trust it.
  • Measure it. Each persona run records whether its "read this one" chapter fits its budget; the Personas section shows it.

The developer has half of this already: "Short of time? Read chapter N first" on both pages. The proposal turns that line into the ask itself.

What is the editor's: the default (twenty minutes), and whether the ten-minute reader is asked at all.

How they work

  • When they run: at a major change to what readers see (a new review page, a new release candidate), or when someone has a question for them; not on a timer.
  • The review pages: each persona opens its page in a real browser on its own device; every request leaving the page is blocked and listed.
  • The book: each persona reads the chapters chosen for its kind of reader, in its own voice, and answers an exit interview.
  • Everything is in the repository: review/synthetic/.
