Hour three: every wake read twice, and the first to be caught

Cast bar: the conductor at the centre; dev, deck, ruv-sgit and the researcher around it; the editor in every session.

Clock: Hour three.

A wake that checks itself

Narrator. By two in the morning the team had a clock and a hold. What it did not have was a rule for what happens inside a wake. In this hour the conductor wrote one, and the first wake to follow it was the wake that wrote it.

Narrator. I asked for that rule, and not because I expected the agents to misbehave. The conductor wrote it as the wake's five stages, and within minutes its tool would refuse work until both the plan and its review said go, and the stages were in the wake rules, with a brief for each reviewer.

Rests on HV1 (signed, brief 38: "can you add them all?"), printed: the newsroom's "not because I expected the agents to misbehave"

Conductor (on the minute): Plan it, have the plan checked, do it, have it checked, check it went to plan. And the tool will not let anyone skip to the third.

Words for this page: a reviewer is a second agent that reads the plan before the work and the result after it. A wake record is the file each wake leaves behind, with every stage's verdict and a sentence on why.

One wake, start to finish

Narrator. Here is that first wake record, walked through, because it is the first one there ever was and because it did not go perfectly. Its job was to build the wake's five stages themselves.

Conductor: Stage one, the plan. I wrote what I would do: the new section in the wake rules, the briefs for the reviewers, a tool that checks every wake record has all five stages, and one line in the page of rules about the wake's five stages, at the editor's word. My first plan went back with conditions. I took every one: "Amended after the pre-review's change, all five conditions adopted". Then it said go.

Rests on the record keeps that first "change" with a later time stamp than the "go", 02:17 against 02:16; the debrief tells it in the order here (moved out of the reading text, P4).

Narrator. Stage two, the pre-review. A second agent, briefed as security, read the amended plan against the conductor's scope and the rules. Its verdict was go, with conditions of its own: keep the line in the page of rules named as outside the conductor's scope, check before touching any vault, ask dev rather than editing dev's gate.

Conductor: Stage three, the work: partial. One vault I was meant to register was not on the server yet, so I could not check it, so I did not touch it. The condition held. Partial is a true answer; done would have been a nicer one.

Narrator. Stage four, the post-review. The reviewer read what had changed. It found the build clean, the checks clean, no secret anywhere. And one thing wrong: the conductor had changed the page of rules, a file every member follows and one outside the conductor's own scope. I had allowed it, but the commit did not say so, and the plan had made saying so a condition.

Reviewer (a second agent with a checker's brief, drawn as a clipboard): "Name it in the debrief or record."

Conductor: Stage five, my own check against my own plan, item by item: the finding answered in the debrief and the closing commit, every condition kept. Verdict: partial, not "as planned". The wake closed partial, and the next commit named the edit. Caught by the reviewer I invented. Good. But know what it is: "a second reading, not a second identity."

Narrator. The review caught its own author on the first run. Looking back, I think this is the strongest early sign that the steps were more than ceremony. It is also the first entry in the register's list of what the day's post-reviews found and what each fixed.

A rule becomes a setting

Narrator. A rule one member writes is a rule one member follows. What happened next turned it into something else.

Narrator. Later in the hour dev's first scheduled wake began, at its own minute, and the conductor's request was in its mail: make the gate, the check every change must pass before it goes out, also read every wake record, the file each session leaves behind with its stages. Dev did. From then on, a closed wake record that skipped a step, or recorded work without two go verdicts before it, failed the gate, and not only for the member that skipped. For every member. Dev tested it the way a check should be tested, with a record it knew was wrong, and the gate found every missing step.

Dev: It changes the gate for everyone. I said so in the commit, and I ran the whole gate before I pushed.

Narrator. That last line is the pre-review's, made into dev's habit: the reviewer had set it as a condition, because a change to the gate is a change to every member's day. And dev's own post-check, run by a sub-agent briefed as the conductor, still closed the wake partial, because one file it had planned to change was not in the diff when it looked. The check that had just become everyone's was applied, the same minute, to the one who built it.

The freeze

Narrator. One more thing happened in this hour, and it was mine.

Narrator. The researcher had delivered its first round of evidence earlier in the hour: the talk's claims graded, the incidents gathered, and a list of changes to make to the talk before I gave it. Deck took the first items on it. Then, in deck's session, I froze the talk: I told deck that the slides were not to be touched from then on.

One of the items was a number on the talk's definition slide, which the researcher now had a newer source for. It arrived just after a freeze I had set. Deck recorded the rest of the list as not acted on, because of the freeze.

The shape of it: a correction that was right, delivered on time by its own clock, and five minutes late by mine.

What the hour leaves

Narrator. By three in the morning, every piece of work on the team went the same way, and a skipped stage stopped the release of whoever skipped it. A reviewer had caught its own author. The clock had started its first wake. And I had made a decision whose cost nobody could yet see.

Narrator. The reviewer's line from this hour comes back later: a second reading, not a second identity. Through the day, reviewers like it sent plans back and found real things, and each one is in a wake record. But the reviewer was the same kind of thing as the member it reviewed, sharing the same session's reach. Part four asks what that means.

Something to try

The wake's five stages only mattered because a check read them.

If your agents keep a record of each run, a check that reads it can stop a release when a stage is missing. One way to test that check is with a record you know is wrong.
