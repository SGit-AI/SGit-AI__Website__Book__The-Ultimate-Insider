Slides as a compromise

Cast bar: the editor, alone. The nine are greyed: they have not arrived yet.

The old way

Narrator. I have been trying to present better for a long time. My slides were always a compromise. I usually read my talks, with the slides behind me as a backdrop, and I knew they could not do what I needed. They do not move, and they do not answer back. So I spent my talks explaining what the slides could not show: this goes from here to there, this grows, this breaks. The room had to imagine the movement in their heads while I described it. I talked over my own pictures.

Narrator. And I always wrote my slides in the last hours or days before a talk, because that is when my thinking came together and my flow arrived. So for years I kept building tools to present with, chasing one thing: a feedback loop while I was still creating, so I could see the idea move, fix it, and see it again before I stood up.

Narrator. For this talk it worked. I had been working with agentic workflows for a while, setting AI agents to work through a job step by step on their own, and the moment lined up. It was one of those loops I would have stopped before. This time I saw it working, and I kept going.

Narrator. The talk before this one, built for ruv.sgit.ai, had come out well and given me the confidence to try. And a lot of the technology this talk ran on was first built there: the player I pressed play on, and the first pieces the team ported into this talk, came from ruv.sgit.ai.

Words on this page: an agent, here, is an AI that works in a loop: it reads, decides, acts with tools, and checks the result. The player is the part of the talk's site that let me press play on stage.

Pressing play

Narrator. On 29 October I gave a talk where I mostly did not have to do that. When a thing had to move, I pressed play and it moved.

Figure: the player I pressed play on: the timer against my time, the steps that build one at a time, my notes.

Figure: the building, mid-run: a house of rooms, ordinary users walking through and stopping, and the agents spreading everywhere, still going.

Narrator. That is the building, an animation the team made for the talk. Ordinary users come in, go where they are supposed to go, and now and then one finds the gold that is not supposed to be there. But they tend to stop. Agents come in far greater numbers, and they keep going until they find it. As I said in the talk, that is the big difference: the agent does not stop. I did not have to explain the movement. I let it run, and talked about what it meant.

Narrator. And later in the talk, not an animation at all: a page I had used for real, the one where I reviewed each agent's policy before it could work. When I onboarded the team that built the talk, each agent asked for what it wanted, and I approved it, changed it or questioned it, and what I agreed became its mandate. I put that page on the screen and said what it was. The room saw a real policy page, not my description of one. Those were the team's Agent Behaviour Policies, the format RiskMandate uses to write down what an agent is for and what it can reach; with SHarness, they are a lot of what I do.

Figure: the policy review page as it was on the screen: one agent's policy, item by item, with a verdict beside each. One line on the page says I had agreed all ten policies; the files say eight of ten by the end of the day.

Narrator. And one more, about twenty minutes in: a Wardley map, the kind of picture I would have struggled to explain with a still, because what matters in it is where things move to. Wardley maps are the best thing I have learned in the last decade; it is how I think. A Wardley map is a picture of a system: what a user needs at the top, what that depends on below it, and how settled each part is, from left to right. You map the system, this needs this, which needs this, and then you map how each part evolves, from genesis to custom built, to product, to commodity.

Narrator. You will see this kind of map again in part two, drawn for the factory itself.

What I would have had to say instead

Narrator. Take the building. Here is what the slide would have been, and what I would have had to say over it.

Figure: the building as a still slide: a box of rooms, a few dots inside, an arrow, and a label saying "agents". The still I would have shown.

Narrator. And over that still, something like this: these dots are ordinary users; they wander in and do what they are supposed to; now and then one finds something it should not, and it stops; now imagine far more of them, and imagine they never stop. Four sentences of narration, for one picture, and the room would still be imagining the movement instead of seeing it.

Something to try

The ideas that are hardest to put on a slide are usually the ones that move.

If you have one you keep explaining by hand, try writing that movement down in a single line.

It makes a good first job for an animation, or for your first agent.
