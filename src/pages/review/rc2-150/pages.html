Narrator. I have been trying to present better for a long time. My slides were always a compromise. I usually read my talks, with the slides behind me as a backdrop, and I knew they could not do what I needed. They do not move, and they do not answer back. So I spent my talks explaining what the slides could not show: this goes from here to there, this grows, this breaks. The room had to imagine the movement in their heads while I described it. I talked over my own pictures.

Narrator. And I always wrote my slides in the last hours or days before a talk, because that is when my thinking came together and my flow arrived. So for years I kept building tools to present with, chasing one thing: a feedback loop while I was still creating, so I could see the idea move, fix it, and see it again before I stood up.

Narrator. For this talk it worked. I had been working with agentic workflows for a while, setting AI agents to work through a job step by step on their own, and this time everything lined up. On other days I would have stopped at the first version, because it was late and it was good enough. This time I could see the loop working, so I kept going.

Narrator. It did not come from nowhere. The talk before this one, built for ruv.sgit.ai, had come out well and given me the confidence to try. And a lot of the technology this talk ran on was first built there: the player I pressed play on, and the first pieces the team ported into this talk, came from ruv.sgit.ai. This is a continuum, not a lucky day.

Narrator. I want to be careful with that word, because it sounds grander than it is. Most of the parts were not new. The build, the check every change had to pass, the brief: each came from something I had built before. The brake that could stop them all was made that night. What was new was having all of it at once, as a team, on one deadline.

Figure: the factory in action: the nine at work around one objective, the clock above them, the brake within reach.

Narrator. You will meet them in the order they arrived: the first four within an hour of an empty project, the rest through the day. And one chair stays empty all day. I planned a security agent and never staffed it. That was more coincidence than decision, but looking back I think it says something good: this project never needed one. Part of running a team like this is knowing which engines to start; you do not fire them all up every time. The empty chair comes back later, and it matters there.

Narrator. So this book is about the factory. The talk was what it happened to make that day. If you have an idea you keep talking over, the factory is the part you can take home.

Narrator. Why only a day? Because I think that last part, taking it apart, is the interesting one. We tend to think of a team, human or not, as something you build to last, and so we build it slowly and carefully. This one was meant to be temporary: assembled quickly around one objective, from parts I already had, and taken apart at the end. That let me build it fast, without the ceremony a permanent team needs, as long as everything it did was written down. The writing down is what made this book possible, and it is also what lets you rebuild it, which is what part five does.
