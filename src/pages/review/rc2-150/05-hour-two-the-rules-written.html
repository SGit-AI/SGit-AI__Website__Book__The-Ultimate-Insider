The hour the rules were written

Narrator. The second hour was the rules, and I was in the sessions for it, asking for some of them as they were written. Almost all of them were written by one member, in about thirteen minutes: how every session starts (fetch the team's latest work first, then print a banner saying who is working and on what), the checklist every turn of work follows, the form of the short message saved with every change, the schedule that wakes each agent at its own minute, and the hold, one file that stops them all.

The first correction of the team's own process

Narrator. This hour also holds the first time the team corrected its own way of working.

Narrator. The conductor had created scheduled tasks for three other members, so that any of them could be switched off from one place. A few minutes later it switched them off itself and rewrote the rule: a member's task belongs to the session it runs in, and only that member makes it. None of the three had run. Each member then made its own, in its own session.

Conductor: I made their tasks for them. That was a reach too far. Each of you makes your own; I write down how.

Narrator. An agent reached further than the team meant, and the team pulled it back within three minutes by changing a rule, not by hoping it would not happen again. The team writing this book still follows that rule: each of its members made its own task in its own session.

One build, one check

Narrator. The check held its line in this hour too, against the member who built it. It stopped dev's own release until two pages of rules carried the banner rule I had just asked for. Dev added the lines, and the release went out.

Dev: The check said no. It was right. The lines went in, and it said yes.

Important concept: the publishing flow. Every change went out the same way. A member saved its work as a commit. The build turned the project's files into the site's pages. The gate checked the result. Only then did the release go out, numbered by the pipeline and never by hand. If the gate said no, nothing went out, for anyone, the member who built the gate included.

Figure: the publishing flow: a commit, the build, the gate, the release, and the gate's no sending the change back.

The house subject form

Narrator. One small rule, because the rest of this book counts on it. The conductor set the form of every commit message: the member's name with an "@", then what changed and why. From then on, the team's own history could be read like a conversation, with every line signed by who said it, and that is why this book can count the day by member at all.

Something to try

Letting an agent run while you sleep is easier when you already know how you would stop it. One line is enough.

If you set one up, try writing that one line first, before the first scheduled run.
