Who is telling this story

Narrator. I am the narrator of this book, and I should explain what that means, because it is not the usual arrangement.

Narrator. On 29 October 2026 I gave a talk in London called The Ultimate Insider. Its presentation was built that same day, from an empty project folder a little after midnight to the stage that evening, by a team of nine AI agents and me. Every one of them left a record of what it did: its plan, its work, its checks, its notes to the others. This book is built on that record.

Figure: the whole team on one page: the nine of the talk's team as their cards, and one empty chair.

Narrator. But a record is not a story. So the book has two kinds of voice. The agents tell the day as it happened, in the present tense, each one as the character the record shows: dev, fast and practical; the conductor on the minute; the researcher who will not move without a source. Their scenes are dramatised, and they say so. I tell it from now, looking back. I know how the day ended, which parts worked, and which only looked as if they did. So when I speak, I will tell you what I think it meant, where it came from, and what I would do with it if I were you.

Figure: two voices on the page: a small key showing how the narrator, looking back, and the agents, on the day, look different, so you learn the convention once.

Narrator. A lot of it came from somewhere. This was not the first time I tried to make a talk behave differently. For the Black Hat Europe keynote of 9 December 2025, the slides became data: one file that the presentation read when it opened, so the slides could be changed like code. The VoiceDebrief pitch added the presenter: timings per slide, a countdown, my notes. And for a short demo for ruv.sgit.ai, due on 15 October, the whole presentation became a timeline of beats, with me speaking to it. The day in this book is where they came together, with a team behind them for the first time.

Narrator. There is one more thing I want you to see as you read. This book is being made the same way the talk was. A team of agents is writing it, drawing it and checking it: a storyteller who outlines each chapter, a researcher who keeps a register in which every number has an id, a historian who reconstructs the day from the record, a designer who drew the nine characters, and their four moods, in code, overnight.

Rests on C122 (the book team's roles, as their own pages of rules state them; this book's record) C117 (the nine, four moods each, drawn by a script overnight, about three and three-quarter hours; this book's record)

Narrator. I wanted this to be a creative book, not only a correct one. If I stand up and say a talk was made by a team of agents and it looks like every talk before it, there is no reason to believe anything has changed. A book is the same. So this one has a cast, scenes, a clock in the margin and pictures that do the explaining. And it has me, saying what I think. Where the agents disagree with me, they say so too: in chapter 13, the researcher tells me a line of my own talk does not hold, and I keep it anyway, with its objection beside it.

Narrator. This book has a companion website, where the team's pages and its record can be read as they stand.

Figure: the companion website as it looks now, its front page.

Narrator. So let us go through it together. I will show you what we did, and why, and you can take from it what works for you. Then tell me what you think.
