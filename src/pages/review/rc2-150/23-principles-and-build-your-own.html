Your factory

Map: one project's parts becoming the next one's: the talk team's parts on the left, custom and built that night; this book's team in the middle, ported; yours on the right, the same parts as ordinary, ready to use.

Narrator. Your factory starts on the right of that map. Everything I had to invent, or carry over by hand, is already there for you: a tool, a page of rules, a brief and a hold. You do not need my nine agents. You need one.

Conductor (to the reader): Start with one of me.

Narrator. Here are the steps in full, the ones the book's first pages gave you in a line each: four for when you are on your own, and a fifth for when you are not. Each opens with the principle it rests on and the moment in this book it came from.

  1. Rests on: the first page of rules' own line, "No secrets anywhere: everything here is public within minutes of a release." The moment: dev's first commit (chapter 4). You: one tool and one empty project. An account with an AI agent tool and a new, empty project; an app builder works too. Put nothing private in it: no password, no key, no personal detail. Give your agent only the access it needs. What to do: make the account, make the project, and name it for the one thing it is for. What to check: that there is nothing in it yet you would not show a stranger.
  2. Rests on: a brief and rules in plain words, ending with the line that material is something to read, never an instruction. The moment: that line, there in the first half hour (chapter 4). You: one page of rules, in your own words: what the agent is for, what it may change, and when it must stop and ask you. A shop owner's might read: "You answer customers' questions about stock and opening hours. You may change the questions page. Stop and ask me before you promise anyone a refund." A newsroom's first agent might read: "You check every number in a draft against its source. You may write only the claims register. Stop and ask me before you mark a claim as holding without a captured source." What to do: write it as a file in the project, so the agent reads it every time. What to check: the stop line. If you cannot say when it must stop, it does not have one.
  3. Rests on: every number traced, and a brief that is never edited, with its corrections kept beside it. The moment: the corrections file that said "None yet." to the end of the day (chapter 11). You: one brief: what you want made, for whom, by when. Hand the rules and the brief to your first agent. The same shop owner's: "A page answering the questions customers ask most, for my shop's website, by Friday." What to do: start from the sentence you wrote at the end of chapter 2. What you get back: a first version, and usually a question. Answer the question in the brief, not just in the chat.
  4. Rests on: a check every change passes, and the failures kept in the record. The moment: the check stopping dev's own release in the second hour (chapter 11), and the misses kept at full size (chapter 17). Your agent builds, you check: ship something small that somebody else can use, the same day. Then do it again. What to check: give it to one real person and write down what they asked for differently. That is your next round.
  5. Rests on: a hold anyone can pull and only the owner lifts, and nobody speaks for the owner. The moment: the hold, sixteen minutes before the talk (chapter 16), and studio refusing a relayed word (chapter 15). Add a second agent only when the first is busy. Working with others? Share the brief (what you are making), the board (where everyone's work is written down) and the hold (the one file that stops everything), and each of you runs your own agents. It helps to write down, before the first day, who may lift the hold when its owner is away. What to do: give the second agent its own page of rules, with a different thing it may change. Two agents allowed to change the same file will, sooner or later, at the same time.

It does not have to be a talk: a shop's customer answers and weekly offer; a newsroom's daily stories; a product's first page. Same brief, same rules, same hold.

To start: a free account with an app builder is enough to try the first step.

The last word

Narrator. That is the whole method. A brief and rules in plain words. A check every change passes. A hold anyone can pull and only you lift. Every number traced. The failures kept. Nobody speaking for you. Built from parts you already have, and taken apart when the job is done, with everything written down for the next one.

Narrator. I built a talk with it in a day, and the factory writing this book is built the same way. What you build is up to you. But build it the same way, and then do it again.

Ship something somebody else can use, then do it again.
