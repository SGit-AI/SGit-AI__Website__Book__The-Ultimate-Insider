Part 4. The factory is stopped.

The team racing its own locks

Clock: Hour nineteen.

Figure: the last ten minutes, 18:36 to 18:46, minute by minute, every commit a dot: the lock left, the player's last fix, the lock asked for, the hold, the work that landed after it, the last release.

Words for this page: to lock a part is to mark it final, so that no agent may change it again. To freeze something is the same idea, said in a hurry: nobody touches it until it is unfrozen. A wake, as in part 2, is one turn of work by one agent.

Narrator. Part 4 is where the factory stops. Here is what it was doing in the hour before I stopped it.

Narrator. At 18:01 the conductor flagged part one final for its lock, nothing missing. Deck put the title slide in front of it at 18:14. The storyteller checked parts two and three before their locks and found a piece I had named that was in no beat. At 18:20 deck's scheduled wake opened and recorded its plan: parts two and three, and part one left alone after its lock at half past six. At 18:21 the conductor flagged part one ready for its lock a second time.

Conductor (on the minute): Part one: final, nothing misses it. Ready for its lock.

Narrator. Meanwhile I was in the player. Its commits in that half hour carry my own times in their subjects, and each one is a fix I asked for and got.

The hold

Clock: Hour nineteen.

Narrator. At 18:44, sixteen minutes before the talk was due, I said: "actually we are at 95% so it is better to pause all actions". (record)

Narrator. The conductor wrote one file. Here it is, as it was written:

"# HOLD: the editor's usage limit is at 95%; pause all actions" (record)

>

"What lifts it: the editor deletes this file and re-enables the paused tasks. No member lifts it." (record)

Narrator. While that file exists, every member's next wake stops before it starts. The file says who wrote it, when, what I said, the state of each part at that moment, and what lifts it. One line names the only person who can.

Conductor (at ninety-five, then calm): I could keep going. That is exactly why I am stopping.

Narrator. What was already in flight still landed. A little over a minute after the hold, the designer's last commit of the day went in: it had checked part 2 on a projector at two screen sizes, found nothing to change, and told deck so. It touched nothing in the slides. Two minutes after the hold, the day's last release went out.

Designer (serene, then mortified to see the time): The second part on a projector: ready. Nothing to change. Ah. The hold.

The locks overtaken from outside

Narrator. One more thing about this hour. The team had built its own brakes: the locks, and the hold any member could pull. Its own locks lost the race. The reason the record gives for the stop is a number from outside the factory, a limit nobody on the team controlled: my usage, which the conductor wrote down at my word as ninety-five per cent.

Narrator. That is the first account, the day's own. I have given two more since, both after the day.

Narrator. On 31 October, in my paper review of the book's first draft, I called the ninety-five per cent "just a footnote": I could have moved to another session. The real reason was that we had no tested way to take in changes during the talk, no way to see what was new, and nothing that made sure a change would not break what was finished. That is why I had been thinking about locking the first version.

Narrator. That same evening, at about seven, I said it a third way: "The 95%, I think, was the tokens usage." (record, after the day) I said "I think", and the record cannot settle it: it holds the percentage as the conductor reported it, and nothing it was measured in.

Narrator. The book keeps all three: the record's, and my two after the day. Chapter 17 takes the second one up as a miss.
