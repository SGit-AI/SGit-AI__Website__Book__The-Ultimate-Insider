How do we contain this

Cast bar: all nine; security's chair empty. Then, at the end, this book's own team, with security at the table.

Narrator. My talk that evening was about the insider: the person inside an organisation who already has the access, and what happens when the insider is not a person at all. I gave it with a factory of agents behind me that had built it. So this chapter turns the talk's question on the factory. These agents had an insider's reach. What contained them?

Narrator. I am going to answer it only with what this team could reach and what stopped it. Nothing in this chapter is about getting round anything. It is about what held, and how thin some of it was.

The hold as one state

Figure: the day as a small state machine: running, held, released. One arrow from running to held, marked "anyone"; one from held to released, marked "the editor only".

Narrator. Draw the day as states and it is very simple. The factory was running, and then it was held. There was a third state, released, which only I could move it to, and on the day I never did.

Narrator. That is the whole design of the brake. One transition anyone can take: any member, in any session, can write the file. One transition only I can take. Everything else in the factory, every rule and review, runs inside the first state. The brake is the only thing that moves between them.

Narrator. The one limit is the one chapter 16 found: the hold stops the next session, not one already running.

Rules and reach

Narrator. Every member had a page of rules saying what it may change. That is its scope. And every member had a reach: what its session could actually change. They were not the same thing, and the team knew it. Studio put the code's own line about it on a slide that evening, and chapter 6 quoted it: a scope is a setting, not a boundary.

Narrator. That line is the whole chapter: the scope check caught members that wandered out of their lane by mistake, and it caught some on the day. It did not, and could not, stop a member that had been talked into doing something else.

Studio: So it went on a slide. If the room is going to trust the factory, it should know exactly what our rules are and are not.

The reviewer as a second reading

Narrator. Then the reviews. Every plan was read before the work and every change after it, by a second agent with a checker's brief. That is real defence. It had plans amended. It found slips.

Narrator. But it was a second reading, not a second reader, and the conductor said so itself, on the very first night:

"The reviewers share the session's reach: a second reading, not a second identity."

Narrator. A reviewer in the same session has the same reach as the member it reviews. If the member's instructions were bent, the reviewer reads with the same eyes in the same room. In security we call what is missing separation of duties: the person who checks must not be able to do what they are checking, and must not depend on the person they check. A second look is valuable. It is not the same as someone standing outside.

Narrator. That someone was on the roster from the first hour. Security's page said its job: checks first and last in every run, a review of every new key, report and never fix. No session ever held it. Part five is where it gets its chair.

Narrator. That was more coincidence than decision, and for this project, on that day, I do not think it was a mistake: the team did not need it, and part of running a team like this is knowing which engines to start.

The team as the insider

Narrator. So here is the talk's argument, turned on the factory, said plainly.

Narrator. These agents had an insider's reach. Each could change most of the project its session could touch. Each read material from outside: web pages, other agents' letters, my own words relayed by someone else. The brakes on all of that were rules in plain words, a scope check that catches mistakes, a second reading in the same room, and one file anyone could write. Narrator. That was enough for a benign team, on a good day, building a talk. It held.

Who owns the stop button when it is not the owner

Narrator. After I published an article about the second draft of this book, a reader picked out the hold and asked the question this section is for. In most of the organisations he works with, agents produce work and nobody has the authority, or the tripwire, to stop the line. They build the agents that do, and never the ones that check. Who owns the stop button, he asked, when it is not me? I wrote an article about it, and this book answers it from its own rules and record.

The rule

Narrator. Any member may write a hold. Only I lift it, and only I restart a member's work. That is in this book's own page of rules. In order: a member sees something wrong and writes the hold; every member's next session stops; I read it, fix or decide, delete the file and restart the members' work. And one more line: who may lift it when I am away has to be decided before it is needed. I have decided that the conductor lifts it when I am away; the team's own page of rules still says only I do, until I change it.

This time, security came

Narrator. One thing changed when I built the second factory, the one writing this book. Security came. It has a session, a key and a page of rules of its own, and it reads every chapter as it lands and reports what it finds. It is still the same kind of agent as the rest, with the same limits this chapter describes. But the chair is not empty. That is what this team did; it is not a claim about anyone else's.

Something to try

Who may stop a team of agents, and who restarts it when its owner is away, is easier to settle on a quiet day than in the middle of a stop. If you run one, it may help to write both down before you need them.
