A second team, reading cold

Cast bar: this book's team on one side of the page; on the other, a second team drawn in outline, the Cold-Read team, finished; between them, one arrow pointing one way.

Narrator. Everything in this book so far was read by one team: this one, with its briefs, its timeline, its register and its reviews.

Narrator. So I set up a second team, in a separate repository, and gave it only the talk's record as it stood at the end of the day, and the note that started it. Nothing of this book's work: no timeline, no register, no chapter, no member in common. It read the record cold. In security this is the idea behind an independent audit. This chapter is what that second reading found, and what it means.

The one-way valve

Narrator. One rule first, said plainly, because the whole value of the second reading rests on it. Material flowed one way. This team reads the blind team's published record. Nothing from this book ever went to it: no brief, no chapter, no decision, no finding. The only thing that ever crossed was the hand-off that started it, and that hand-off is kept, whole, in this repository, so anyone can check what the blind team was given. The blind team is finished now. This chapter speaks about it, never to it.

What it means

Narrator. Chapter 18 gave the conductor's own words for its reviewers, and the difference between a second reading and a second identity.

Narrator. The blind team is the nearest this book has to a second identity. It shared nothing with this team but the record itself and the hand-off: no session, no timeline, no member. And it saw six things this team had missed, and every time and commit both of us cite agreed to the second. That is a finding about this book and this record, not a claim about every review. Chapter 18 left a question open: what stands outside the team? This answers it. Here, for one reading of one day, something did. Part five is where the sessions end, and the record stays.
