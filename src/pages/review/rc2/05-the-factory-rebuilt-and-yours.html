Taken apart

Narrator. That night the factory was taken apart.

Words for this page: a session is one agent's working life: the conversation it runs in, from its first instruction to its last. When a session ends, only what it wrote down survives.

Narrator. So here is what went, and what stayed. The sessions went: the nine agents, as running things, were gone. What stayed was everything they had written down. Every page of rules. Every wake record, with its plan, its reviews and its verdicts. Every debrief. Every mistake, in the agent's own words. The talk itself, and the record of how it was made.

Narrator. That is the part people miss when they hear "a team of agents for a day". The team is temporary. The record is not. And the record is what the next factory is built from.

The third, planned

Narrator. Now I am planning a third, bigger one, and it shares three things between people: one brief, one board where the work is written down, and one hold. It is for a team I am taking to Web Summit, the technology conference in Lisbon: several people, each to run their own agents. It has not run yet. The parts are the same as the first factory's.

Conductor (pacing, to the reader): Same clock. Same hold. More hands on it.

Narrator. Here is how I expect the three shared things to work day to day, written as a plan, because that is what it is.

Narrator. The brief has one owner, who writes it and changes it. Everyone else reads it, and asks for changes in writing. Two people editing one brief is how a team ends up with two briefs.

Narrator. The board is read every morning by everyone, before their own agents start. Each person's agents write their work there, so nobody has to ask what anybody else did overnight.

Narrator. The hold can be pulled by anyone, human or agent. Who may lift it is written down before the first day: the owner of the brief, and one named person when the owner is away. Until that is written, nobody lifts it.

Your factory

Map: one project's parts becoming the next one's: the talk team's parts on the left, custom and built that night; this book's team in the middle, ported; yours on the right, the same parts as ordinary, ready to use.

Narrator. Look at where your factory sits on that map. Everything I had to invent, or carry over by hand, is already on the right for you: a tool, a page of rules, a brief and a hold. You do not need my nine. You need one.

Conductor (straight to the reader): Start with one of me.

Narrator. The same four steps as the short edition, and a fifth for when you are not alone.

  1. You: one tool and one empty project. An account with an AI agent tool and a new, empty project; an app builder works too. Put nothing private in it: no password, no key, no personal detail. Give your agent only the access it needs. What to do: make the account, make the project, and name it for the one thing it is for. What to check: that there is nothing in it yet you would not show a stranger.
  2. You: one page of rules, in your own words: what the agent is for, what it may change, and when it must stop and ask you. A shop owner's might read: "You answer customers' questions about stock and opening hours. You may change the questions page. Stop and ask me before you promise anyone a refund." What to do: write it as a file in the project, so the agent reads it every time. What to check: the stop line. If you cannot say when it must stop, it does not have one.
  3. You: one brief: what you want made, for whom, by when. Hand both to your first agent. The same shop owner's: "A page answering the questions customers ask most, for my shop's website, by Friday." What to do: start from the sentence you wrote at the end of chapter 1. What you get back: a first version, and usually a question. Answer the question in the brief, not just in the chat.
  4. Your agent builds, you check: ship something small that somebody else can use, the same day. Then do it again. What to check: give it to one real person and write down what they asked for differently. That is your next round.
  5. Add a second agent only when the first is busy. Working with others? Share the brief, the board and the hold, and each of you runs your own agents. Write down who may lift the hold when its owner is away; until you have, nobody does. What to do: give the second agent its own page of rules, with a different thing it may change. Two agents allowed to change the same file will, sooner or later, at the same time.

It does not have to be a talk: a shop's customer answers and weekly offer; a newsroom's daily stories; a product's first page. Same brief, same rules, same hold.

What it costs: to start, you need a free account; paid plans of the kind the team used start at $20 a month (prices as published on 2 November 2026). The record of my own day holds no figure for what it cost.
