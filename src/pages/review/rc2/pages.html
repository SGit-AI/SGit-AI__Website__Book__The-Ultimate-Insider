The creator's problem · page 1

Cast bar: the editor, alone. The nine are greyed: they have not arrived yet.

Narrator. For years my slides were a compromise. I had an idea to get across, and the slides could not move, so I spent the talk explaining what they could not show: this goes from here to there, this grows, this breaks. I talked over my own pictures.

Narrator. On 1 November, three days after the talk, I recorded a voice note about it, and this is how it came out, unedited: "I had to speak a lot because in a way I had to provide the animation that was missing. I didn't know if I wanted to say, look, here, these things moves from A to B, you know, I kind of had to explain that A to B move". That is the problem in one sentence. The idea was a movement. The slide was a still. I was the bit in between.

Narrator. It sounds small. It is not. Every minute I spend narrating an arrow is a minute the room is listening to me describe a picture instead of thinking about the idea. And the harder the idea, the more of it lives in the movement: how something spreads, where it stops, what changes when you add more of it. Those were exactly the ideas I wanted to get across.

The creator's problem · page 2

Clock: Hour nineteen.

Narrator. On 29 October I gave a talk where I mostly did not have to do that. When a thing had to move, I pressed play and it moved.

Figure: the player as I saw it on stage: the slide large, a countdown against my time, the step controls, and my notes behind a key press ( index/talk-site/shots/talk__player.jpg ).

Narrator. Here is one moment, from the recording. It is an animation the team called the building. In it, ordinary users walk through and stop; agents keep going until they find what should not be there. I did not explain the movement. I let it run, and talked about what it meant:

"This is a really cool animation of what happens. Normal users come in and do things: they go where they're supposed to go. Every now and then one finds the gold that isn't supposed to be there, but they also tend to stop. ... Agents come with far more volume. They also change, going from users to agents, and they eventually find the gold. That's the big difference: the agent doesn't stop."

Narrator. The picture did the explaining, and I said what it meant. That is what I had wanted from slides for years.

Narrator. And here is the part I like most. That animation was not finished in the morning and left alone. I changed it at 18:26, about half an hour before the talk was due to start. I could do that because the thing that made it was still running, and still listening.

The talk ran for about 87 minutes, to a room in London. Its transcript was added to the record after the day.

The creator's problem · page 5

Band: Bret Victor's words as published; the talk named and dated as the register holds it (CUSEC 2012).

Narrator. So what gave me that loop? Not a better slide tool. A factory.

Narrator. By factory I mean something specific, and fairly modest. A team of AI agents, gathered around one objective, run hard for one day, and taken apart when the job was done. Nine of them, each with one job and one page of rules. Most of the parts were not new: the build, the check every change had to pass, the brief. Each came from something I had built before. The brake that could stop them all was made that night. What was new was having all of them at once, as a team.

Words on this page: an agent , here, is an AI that works in a loop: it reads, decides, acts with tools, and checks the result. The player is the part of the talk's site that let me press play on stage.

Narrator. It sounds grander than it is. You will meet each of them in the next chapter, in the order they arrived: the first four within the hour of an empty project, the rest through the day. One chair at the table stays empty all day, and that matters later.

Figure: the cast cards, greyed, in a row, each with its job in one line: dev, the site and its build; the conductor, the clock and the plan; deck, the talk's beats and slides; ruv-sgit, the workshop of experiments; the researcher, every fact checked against its source; the designer, how it looks on the projector; the storyteller, the order of the story; motion, the pieces that move; studio, several versions side by side.

Narrator. This book is about the factory. The talk was what it happened to make that day. If you have an idea you keep talking over, the factory is the part you can take home.

The factory is assembled · page 10

Clock: Hours two to three.

Narrator. Then, in the small hours, the conductor built the machinery. This is the part that let me leave them alone, and I did not know yet how much I would.

Conductor (on the minute) : A clock: each member wakes at its own minute. A hold anyone can pull. And every piece of work goes the same way: plan it, have the plan checked, do it, have it checked, check it went to plan.

Conductor (to the team, a little smug) : Nobody waits to be told. When your minute comes round, you look at what is yours and you do the next thing.

Words for this page: a wake is one turn of work by one agent, on its own clock. A reviewer is a second agent that reads the plan before the work and the result after it. A debrief is the short note an agent writes at the end of each wake.

Narrator. Five steps, in plain words: plan it; have the plan checked; do it; have it checked; check it went to plan. That was the rule for every member, every wake. If a check said stop, the work stopped. It looks slow written down. The checking is what let nobody watch.

Figure: the five-step wake as a loop, with a clock round it: each member's minute marked on the dial.
