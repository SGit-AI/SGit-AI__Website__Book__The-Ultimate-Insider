## Built from its parts

**Narrator.** The next day I built a second factory from the first one's parts, rule for rule, to write the book you are reading. Its first job was to read the first factory's record. It had the same clock, the same five stages, the same hold and the same rule that nobody speaks for me, and new jobs, because a book is not a talk.

## Its own failures, kept

**Narrator.** The rule that the failures stay in is not only for the first factory. Here are the second factory's, at the same size.

### The brake line, refused and corrected

**Narrator.** The first is a refusal, and it was aimed at a line in this very book, written in my voice. An early draft of the shortest version of this book, its first chapter, said that every part of the first factory was old: the build, the check, the brief, the brake. The line came from the short editions, where the storyteller had written it first; the writer carried it into the chapter, in my voice. The researcher traced it, line by line, against the historian's account of where each part came from.

**Researcher** *(to the writer, by letter)*: The chapter holds, but for the brake. The hold was made that night, with the schedule. It was not an old part.

**Writer**: Then the line is wrong. I change it, and I say so in the record.

**Narrator.** The line you read in chapter 2, that the brake was made that night, is the corrected one. The researcher then caught it in the short editions too, and counted it as its own miss, because it had traced those first; the storyteller fixed them. Nobody asked to keep the nicer sentence.

### The evening lost to the usage limit

**Narrator.** The second is a failure of the rule I carried over from the talk's last hour: the conductor holds the team before my usage runs out. A few days after the talk, it did not. The account reached its limit first. Nothing moved again until just after midnight.

**Conductor**: The tasks kept firing, and each wake ended in seconds at the limit. Nothing was harmed, and nothing was left half-done. So I wrote it down for the editor, and asked him how hard we should run at night.

**Narrator.** I told the conductor to keep the pace.

### A third, chosen by the historian

**Narrator.** The historian chose the third, and it chose its own.

**Historian**: I told the storyteller and the writer that the talk's conductor rewrote dev's first page of rules, because I read the two changes in the order of their times, dev's first and the conductor's after it. Chapters rested on it. The record says otherwise. The conductor's change grew from the empty first commit, not from dev's: it wrote a longer page of its own, in parallel, without having seen dev's, and when it merged dev's work, the page that came out was dev's, word for word, with two of the conductor's lines added. The order of the times was right. The order of the work was not. I trusted the order of events over the shape of the record, and I caught it the same day, by reading what each change was built on and not only when it landed. The writer set both chapters right minutes after.

**Narrator.** The lesson it wrote down is the one I would give anyone reading a record, people included: when two changes to one file land minutes apart, read what each was built on before you say which changed whose.

## Something to try

*A second build shows what the first one leaned on without anyone noticing. If you have something you built once, rebuilding it from its parts and its record will show you what the record was missing, which is usually the most useful thing you learn.*
