**Cast bar:** six agents: the conductor, dev, deck, ruv-sgit, the researcher and the designer; the editor's chair empty from just after four until the end of hour fourteen.

**Narrator.** My last word of the night was at 04:03. My next is at about ten to two in the afternoon. In between, nobody started any work but the clock. This chapter goes through those hours one at a time, because the point of them is that they are ordinary, and ordinary is the thing people do not believe until they see it hour by hour.

## Hour six: every wake on the clock

**Clock:** Hour six.

`%% clock record: 05:00 to 05:59 on 29 October; commits 20 (C095, hour 05); wakes all scheduled (C087); clock hours of 29 October (C110; the conductor's CK1).`

**Narrator.** Hour six is the first hour in which every wake in the record was started by the schedule. Not one was mine. Each member woke at its own minute, in slot order, did one step of its queue with its reviews, and closed. The conductor set the pace and wrote the night log's third edition. Deck sent its second experiment to ruv-sgit, and ruv-sgit delivered it within the hour. The designer wrote a third note. Dev built the designer's first.

**Narrator.** One wake closed partial, and said so. **Narrator.** That is a small thing, and it is the thing I would look for first in any team I had left alone. Not whether everything finished. Whether what did not finish was written down as not finished.

**Narrator.** The hour holds one more sign of the schedule working. The designer's first note, written at 03:41, was built by dev at 05:51, through the conductor's schedule: set for dev's slot in hour five, it landed in the one in hour six. **Conductor** *(on the minute, handing over a page)*: What was done, what is ordered, and what only you can decide. In that order.

**Narrator.** It is the first thing the team wrote in order to hand itself back to a person. Read the last part again: what only you can decide. A team that runs on its own still has to know what it may not decide on its own, and say so in writing, at the top of the page.

## Hour fourteen: the empty queue

**Clock:** Hour fourteen.

`%% clock record: 13:00 to 13:59 on 29 October; commits 20 (C095, hour 13); wakes all scheduled (C087); the feedback round at about 13:50 by its brief, committed at 14:07; clock hours of 29 October (C110; the conductor's CK1).`

**Narrator.** Hour fourteen began like the others. Dev drew the conductor's pace file as a flow and said its own work step was partial. The conductor's brief for every pre-review now named the files a member holds, not only the files it may write.

**Narrator.** Then, at 13:40, the first idle wake of its kind. ruv-sgit woke at its minute, looked in its mailroom, and found nothing. No request. It closed idle and said so.

**ruv-sgit** *(looking up from an empty bench)*: Nothing in the mailroom. Everything I made is waiting on him.

**Narrator.** Read that the right way round. The maker had nothing to make, not because the factory had stopped, but because everything it had made was waiting on me. Minutes later deck passed the third version of the picture, ready for me. The factory had done what it could do without its creator, and the empty queue was its way of saying so.

**Narrator.** Not long after ruv-sgit's idle wake, at about ten to two by the brief's own first line, I dictated a round of feedback in a chat, for the conductor's session: the morning review's corrections, the talk's framing, the story of the animations, the player, and a storyteller to join the team. ## What nine hours show

**Narrator.** Nine hours. Every wake started by the clock. Every wake with its five steps. The ones that closed partial said so. One failure of the night came round a third time, and was found, and is kept. Requests answered at the next slot with nobody chasing. Work sent back and redone, three times for one picture. A mistake owned by the member that made it. And at the end, a queue that only one person could clear, and a maker with nothing to make.

**Narrator.** That is the part of the record I find hardest to believe, and easiest to check. Part 3 now turns to how the team did it: the channels it talked through, the rhythm it kept, the plumbing under every scene.
