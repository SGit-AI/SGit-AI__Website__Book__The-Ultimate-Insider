## 1. Verdict

**The arc holds.** The book has a clear spine: a problem (slides cannot move), a method (a temporary factory of agents), a day told hour by hour, a stop, an honest accounting, a containment argument, and a rebuild the reader can copy. The front matter now does what a Leanpub reader needs: "Start here" gives the formula and a map of the five parts with a short route (part five first), "The cast says hello" introduces the nine, and "Who is telling this story" sets up the two voices. The ending lands: the last word on page 114 is a real summary, and the tear-out list of cards is a good closing object.

**Where it sags, repeats or jumps:**

1. **The talk's own argument arrives late.** The book never says, until chapter 18 (page 92), what the talk was about: the insider, and what happens when the insider is not a person. A first reader spends 90 pages watching a team build a talk without knowing its thesis. One paragraph in "Who is telling this story" or chapter 1 fixes this, and it makes chapter 18 a pay-off rather than a reveal.
2. **Two thematic chapters interrupt the chronology in part three.** Chapter 9 ends the long quiet morning (page 56), then chapters 10 ("Talking through git") and 11 ("The plumbing") step outside time, and the story resumes with chapter 12 ("The creator comes back", page 67). The chapters are good; their placement breaks the build-up just before the afternoon accelerates. Either mark them as an interlude ("How the team worked, while nobody watched") or move them to part five, where the book explains how to build your own.
3. **The exit interviews in chapter 6 spoil the ending.** Pages 34 to 37 give every member's end-of-day lessons (including the lock that never turned and the last wakes that never closed) in part two, before the reader has lived the afternoon. Part four then tells the same failures again. Moving the interviews to part four, after chapter 17, turns them from spoilers into a reckoning.
4. **Part four retells.** Chapter 17 says "Chapter 16 told this one" (page 88), "Chapter 8 told this one as it happened" (page 89), and "Chapter 16 told why" (page 87, and again on page 94). The accounting is valuable; telling each failure once, in full, at the point it happens, and keeping chapter 17 as a one-page register of misses, would cut several pages and keep momentum.
5. **"Five steps" means two things.** The front matter's formula is "the five steps" (pages 3 and 113), and the wake's checks are "the five stages" in most places but "five steps" on page 35. A newcomer will merge them. Use "the formula" (or "the five steps") only for the reader's path, and "the five stages" only for the wake, everywhere.
6. **The stop-button answer contradicts itself.** Page 95 says who may lift the hold when Dinis is away "is not yet" written, "so until it is, nobody does". Page 110 and the last word (page 114) say the conductor lifts it when he is away. The newsroom's article answers it the second way. Pick one and make page 95 match.
7. **The third factory is out of date as it stands.** Chapter 22 (page 109) says the Web Summit team "has not run" and "nothing in it is a result". By the Leanpub release it has run for days: people with their own conductors, a shared site, a key for every person, signed append lanes. Either date the chapter clearly ("as planned on 2 November") or add one paragraph on what has happened since, with a link.
8. **Thirteen "Figure to come" placeholders remain** (pages 21, 25, 60, 73, 75, 76, 78, 79, 81, 95, 97, 99, 100). For a wider review release that is fine if the front matter says so; for anything called a release candidate on Leanpub, each should either be drawn or cut.

**What a first Leanpub reader needs that is missing:**

- **The event.** The talk is "in London" on 29 October; hack::soho is named only in the appendix commands. Name the event once.
- **What a behaviour policy is.** The book shows a "policy review page" (page 10), "a policy, in a form a program can check" (page 32) and the talk tree's "Behaviour policies" (page 73), but never names Agent Behaviour Policies, RiskMandate or SHarness, which are the reasons Dinis builds any of this. One sentence and one link would let a reader follow the thread out of the book.
- **Where to go next.** There is no further-reading page. Section 5 proposes one.
- **How to give feedback.** "Then tell me what you think" (page 7) needs a place: an email, the companion site, or the newsroom.

## 2. Part by part

**Front matter (pages 1 to 7).** Strong. "Start here", the map of parts, "The cast says hello", "Zero to presentation" and "Who is telling this story" work as a sequence. Two small points: the formula's step 5 packs board, hold and conductor into one sentence that is easier as two; and page 7's lineage (Black Hat, VoiceDebrief, ruv.sgit.ai) is exactly where links to the newsroom's own accounts belong (section 3).

**Part one, the creator's problem (chapters 1 to 3, pages 8 to 21).** The best-shaped part. Problem, principle (Bret Victor), the loop, what a factory is, then the tour of what it made. Chapter 3 ends with the keys and "the house they live in" (pages 18 to 20), which introduces keys and vaults before the reader needs them; a forward pointer ("chapter 20 and 21 come back to keys") would help. "The day at a glance" (page 21) is a good bridge, but its main figure is still to come, and its commit table (650 in all) is the third different commit count in the book (section 4).

**Part two, the factory is assembled (chapters 4 to 8, pages 22 to 51).** Clear chronology, hour by hour. Chapter 4's first page of rules and chapter 5's "the hold existed before the first scheduled wake did" are the two ideas a reader should leave part two with, and both are well placed. Chapter 6 (pages 32 to 38) is the problem in this part: it starts as an introduction of the roles and turns into end-of-day exit interviews and the security non-interview. Recommendation in section 1, point 3. Chapter 7's walk through one wake (pages 39 to 43) teaches more in five pages than any diagram could. Chapter 8 closes the part well with "six agents, a clock and a brake".

**Part three, the factory runs (chapters 9 to 15, pages 52 to 81).** Chapter 9 earns its slowness ("ordinary is the point"). Chapters 10 and 11 interrupt (point 2). Chapter 12 is the pivot of the whole book: the creator returns, the queue clears, the loop runs; the redrawn cycle (think, explain, create, review) and the OODA comparison (page 70) are the right ideas in the right place. Chapter 13 (the story before the slides, the objection kept) is a set piece that should stay. Chapter 14 (port, not rebuild) and 15 (faster than the clock) carry the build-up to the stop well.

**Part four, the factory is stopped (chapters 16 to 19, pages 82 to 100).** Chapter 16 is the climax and reads like one. Chapter 17 retells (point 4). Chapter 18 is the intellectual centre: the state machine, scope versus reach, a second reading not a second identity, separation of duties, the team as the insider, and the stop-button question. It should be the chapter every link in section 3 points into. Chapter 19 (the cold read) is unusual and valuable; its "one-way valve" is a strong idea that deserves a line in the introduction too.

**Part five, the factory rebuilt, and yours (chapters 20 to 23, pages 101 to 114).** Chapter 20's "a session is not a member" is one of the book's most reusable ideas. Chapter 21 (this book's team) and the making-of page (144) cover similar ground; consider one pointing to the other. Chapter 22 is dated (point 7). Chapter 23 lands: the map from custom to ordinary, the steps in full, the checklist and the last word.

**Back matter (pages 116 to 145).** "The tour, at length" repeats chapter 3 and could live on the companion site. "The words used" is useful. Appendix A (17 pages of numbers and commands) is the book's proof and should stay, but a Leanpub reader would benefit from one line at its head saying who it is for. Appendix B and "How this book was made" are good closers.

## 4. Where the book and the newsroom differ

1. **Three commit counts.** Page 17: "439 by the agents themselves, not counting merges". Page 21's table: 650 commits in all, hours 0 to 18, including 210 releases and 50 with no member named. The newsroom's case study: "814 commits (603 by the agents, 210 release commits by CI, and my first)". They may all be right with different definitions, but a reader who follows the link will see three numbers. Give each a definition in the text, or use one in the narrative and keep the others in Appendix A.
2. **Behaviour policies are unnamed in the book** and are the main thread of the newsroom. The book's "policy" and "policy review page" are the talk team's Agent Behaviour Policies. Naming them once (page 10 or 32) is the bridge.
3. **"Material, never instructions" has moved on.** In the book it is a flat rule. Since 4 November, the Web Summit team has made it checkable: a lane message is material unless it carries a verified signature from the person. The book does not need the mechanism, but chapter 22's third factory is where one sentence would show the rule growing up.
4. **The third factory has run** (point 7 in section 1). The newsroom's Web Summit articles describe people with their own conductors, a shared site and keys for every person.
5. **The stop-button answer** differs inside the book (section 1, point 6). The newsroom's article and the book's own last word say the conductor lifts the hold when the owner is away.
6. **The empty security chair.** The book (page 14) now frames it as "more coincidence than decision", and page 95 shows security at the table in the second factory. The newsroom has not written about security joining the book team; a short article would close that loop.
7. **The reality file** is one of the book's best ideas (page 25: "A thing not listed here does not exist, whatever a brief or an article says.") and has no newsroom article of its own. Worth one.
