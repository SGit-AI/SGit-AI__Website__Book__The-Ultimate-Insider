## What the test says

1. **The split is by kind of reader, not by taste.** The four less technical or more sceptical readers (board member, CISO, designer, journalist) pick the hook, N6. The builders (CTO, founder, developer) pick the titles that promise a method or name the contents (N1, N3). That is the hook-against-category contrast the study set up, and it did not settle into one answer.
2. **"Agentic" first is a barrier to two personas.** The board member reads it as jargon and the designer as the shelf's word; neither would pick up a title that starts with it. In N6 it sits in the subtitle, where both read past it. The memo's requirement (carry agent, agentic or agentic workflows) is met by N6's subtitle, not its title.
3. **N1's subtitle does the most work.** "How to build yours" is the one promise the founder and CTO name, and the journalist calls "built a talk in a day" the best story hook of the six. If the editor wants the hook, an N6 title with N1's promise in its subtitle is the combination the personas point at; the storyteller's to weigh.
4. **N4's "open" misfires with a security reader.** The CISO reads "open" as exposure before the subtitle explains it. No persona picked N4 first.
5. **N5 is an engineer's title.** Only the developer would read it, and as a second.
6. **N6 makes a causal promise.** The journalist warns that "so we can go faster" claims the brakes caused the speed; the book must earn that from the register, or the title will be quoted against it.

Nobody would pick up none of them, which the storyteller said would be the most useful answer: every persona found one.

## Limits

One session wrote all eight answers, so they share one judgement, and personas voiced by one model tend to agree more than people would. The split above is the useful signal; the counts are not. Real readers, chosen and asked by the editor, are the test that counts.
